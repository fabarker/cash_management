Two Different Questions

What a settable grace period would give you that sweep_opening_surplus already doesn't. Short answer: they control different things, and the shipped rule leaves the more dangerous one untouched.

The distinction

One sentence, and everything below is an illustration of it.

sweep_opening_surplus  —  when you must decide
the grace period        —  how long you may still be holding

The sweep forces a surplus to be dealt today. It says nothing at all about when that trade settles — and until it settles, the money is still in your account earning or losing the interest differential. Settlement is what the grace period governs.

They are two dials on two different things, and turning one does not move the other. I assumed when writing this that the sweep would make the grace largely redundant. It does not, and the gap matters more than the overlap.

A plain analogy

The sweep is a rule that says you must post the letter today. The grace period is how many days the post office is allowed to take. Making someone post the letter promptly does not control when it arrives, and if the delivery window is set by something other than your own policy, you have no control over the outcome at all.

1 — How long you're exposed

The sweep is satisfied identically in every row below. The exposure is not.

Scenario S07: the account opens holding 2,500,000 dollars against a trivial 50,000 obligation. The dollar pays 6.40% against sterling's 3.50%, so every day it sits there is money earned — and the whole point of the corridor is that this money is not yours to earn.

With sweep_opening_surplus switched on, and only the grace period varying:

Every row deals on day 0. The sweep is fully satisfied in all of them — the decision is committed today, exactly as required. What changes is the tenor, and therefore how many days the 2,450,000 dollars remain in the account before the sale settles.

The cost falls by about 142 for each extra day of grace, and that fall is the carry being collected. Which is to say: the grace period is the exposure dial, and the sweep does not touch it.

Note the last row. A grace of 3 gives the same answer as 2, because you cannot settle later than the longest tenor your desk quotes. The grace only bites when it is shorter than the settlement window — which is exactly the situation the next section makes dangerous.

2 — The bypass it doesn't close

The most important of the three, and the reason this is worth building.

The grace period is not a setting. It is read off the longest settlement lag in your tenor list, so adding a tenor lengthens it — whether or not anyone intended that, and whether or not the tenor is ever dealt.

Same S07, sweep_opening_surplus still on, adding tenors to the menu:

Read the bottom row against this

Switching the anti-speculation policy off entirely gives a cost of −23.72. Declaring a five-day tenor, with every rule still switched on and the sweep obediently forcing a day-0 decision, gives 9.03. The policy has been almost completely neutralised, and the plan still passes every check.

The sweep did its job perfectly in all four rows. It forced the decision onto day 0 every time. It simply has nothing to say about settling five days later.

So the route to disabling a risk control runs through the market data rather than through the risk settings, and sweep_opening_surplus does not block it. A settable grace period would: you could offer T+5 to the desk and still hold the exposure window at two days, because those would finally be two separate decisions.

3 — Money it never sees

The sweep only ever looks at today. Within a single plan, the grace governs the rest.

A receipt of 900,000 dollars landing on day 1, against a 100,000 obligation on day 5. The account opens flat, so the day-0 surplus the sweep measures is zero — it has nothing to say about this money at all.

GracePlanCostUSD balance by day
0 or 1 daysell on day 1, same-day342.600 · 100k · 100k · 100k · 100k · 0
2 days (shipped)sell on day 2, same-day290.850 · 900k · 100k · 100k · 100k · 0

With a two-day grace the receipt sits in the account overnight — 900,000 dollars held on day 1 with nothing but the grace permitting it. Shorten the grace and it is sold the day it lands.

This one is the mildest of the three, because we established previously that a day-1 receipt becomes today's opening balance at the next morning's re-plan, where the sweep picks it up. So in a rolling process the exposure is bounded at a day. But within any single plan, the grace is the only thing governing it.

What this means

The sweep fixed a real problem: a plan that was optimal every morning and never executed. That problem is gone and nothing here reopens it.

But the exposure question was never the sweep's to answer, and it is currently answered by the length of your tenor list. Today that happens to be two days and the policy is sound. It stays sound only for as long as nobody adds a longer tenor — and adding one is a market-data change, not a risk decision, so it would not naturally attract a policy review.

The change, if you want it

A holding_grace_days setting on Config, defaulting to the longest settlement lag so that nothing changes unless someone chooses it. Small and contained: it is a single number in one function, and the corridor is already the only place that reads it.

Two things worth deciding at the same time. Should it be per-currency — three days on a G10 balance, none on an exotic? And should a grace longer than the tenor list be rejected as a mistake, or accepted as harmless slack? Neither blocks the work.

One caution from the measurements above: a grace of zero is not automatically the safe choice. It forces same-day settlement, which removes the choice of tenor, costs about 284 more on S07, and is infeasible outright against a desk that does not quote T+0.

Every figure here came from solving the model, not from estimating. The grace period was varied independently of the tenor list to isolate it, which is precisely the separation the proposed setting would make permanent.
