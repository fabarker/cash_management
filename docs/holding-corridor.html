The short version

Two numbers per currency per day, and everything else is detail.

For every foreign currency, on every day of the horizon, the corridor sets two limits on the closing balance:

−floor[day]   ≤   balance[day]   ≤   ceiling[day]

The ceiling is the most of that currency you are allowed to be holding. The floor is the deepest you are allowed to be overdrawn. Both are worked out before any trading, from the opening balance and that currency's own expected payments and receipts. Neither depends on what the optimiser decides to do.

The purpose is to stop the model taking a currency position for its own sake. Left alone, an optimiser that notices one currency pays more interest than another will simply buy that currency and sit on it. The corridor says: you may hold what your obligations justify, and no more.

The one thing to carry

The corridor never asks what a trade costs, and never asks whether a plan is a good one. It only asks whether a balance is justified. Everything it forbids, it forbids by making a balance impossible — not by ruling out a trade.

Everything starts here

The do-nothing ladder: what each account would look like if nobody traded.

Take the opening balance in a currency, add that currency's expected payments and receipts day by day, and change nothing else. That running balance is the do-nothing ladder, and it is the raw material for every number in this document.

Here is scenario S07 — an account opening with 2,500,000 dollars and one small dollar payment of 50,000 due on day 5:

S07 — USDD0D1D2D3D4D5
Do-nothing ladder 2,500,0002,500,0002,500,000 2,500,0002,500,0002,450,000

Two things get read off this ladder and used everywhere below:

  • The hole — how far below zero the ladder goes on each day, or zero if it does not. This is money you genuinely do not have and will have to buy.
  • What you were given — the opening balance plus receipts banked so far. No amount of trading can change this, which is what makes it safe to use in a limit.

S07's ladder never goes below zero, so its hole is zero on every day. It has 2,500,000 dollars it was given and one 50,000 obligation. That gap is exactly what the corridor is about.

The ceiling, in three parts

Three separate reasons you might legitimately be holding a currency.

The ceiling is not one idea. It is three, worked out independently and then combined:

PartAnswers the questionComes from
A — to acquire How much of this currency am I short and will have to buy? The hole in the ladder
B — earmark How much of what I was given is already spoken for by a payment still ahead? Receipts banked, capped by outflows remaining
C — grace How much was already sitting there before I could have done anything about it? The ladder itself, for the first few days only

A and B add together. C is not added — it acts as a minimum, lifting the ceiling only if it is larger than the other two combined:

ceiling[day]  =  max(  A[day] + B[day],   C[day]  )

Each of the three is worth taking on its own, because each has a scenario in the library where it is the only one doing anything.

Part A — what you must buy

Scenario S01: no opening balance, no receipts, one payment. Only part A is live.

You owe 750,000 dollars on day 4 and hold none. The hole in the ladder is the amount you will have to buy, so the ceiling permits exactly that much and nothing else.

S01 — USDD0D1D2D3D4D5
Do-nothing ladder000 0−750,000−750,000
A — to acquire000 0750,000750,000
B — earmark000 000
C — grace000 000
CEILING000 0750,000750,000

Read the zeros on days 0 to 3. You are not permitted to hold a single dollar before day 4, because until then you have no dollar obligation to justify it. Any purchase must therefore settle on day 4 — the day the money leaves.

This is where the setting lives

How far ahead part A looks is the one genuinely adjustable piece of policy in the corridor. As shipped it looks at the day itself and nothing else, which is why days 0 to 3 are zero above. Turn settle_on_need_only off and it instead looks at the day plus the longest settlement lag, so the day-4 hole becomes visible from day 2 and the ceiling opens earlier.

The stricter setting means the currency is never held overnight — bought for value on the day it is paid away. It costs the interest a short holding would have earned: S01 is 1,126.07 with the wider window and 1,135.87 with the narrow one, and the 9.80 difference is exactly two days of dollar interest.

Part B — what is spoken for

Scenario S04: a receipt arrives that a later payment will consume. Only part B is live.

1,400,000 dollars arrive on day 1. A payment of 950,000 falls due on day 5. The ladder never goes below zero, so part A is zero throughout — there is nothing to buy. Without part B the ceiling would be zero as well, and the model would be forced to sell the receipt and buy it back to make the payment: two commissions and two spreads, for nothing.

S04 — USDD0D1D2D3D4D5
Do-nothing ladder01,400,0001,400,000 1,400,0001,400,000450,000
A — to acquire000 000
B — earmark0950,000950,000 950,000950,000950,000
C — grace01,400,0000 000
CEILING01,400,000950,000 950,000950,000950,000

The earmark is the smaller of two figures, and both caps matter:

B[day] = min(  payments still ahead of you,   money you have actually been given so far  )
  • Capped by payments ahead so you cannot earmark more than you will actually spend. On day 1 there is 950,000 still to pay, so only 950,000 of the 1,400,000 is protected. The other 450,000 is surplus and must be swept.
  • Capped by what you were given so the earmark cannot be used to manufacture a position. Before the money arrives, it protects nothing — which is why day 0 is zero even though a payment exists on day 5. Trading cannot inflate this figure, so it cannot be gamed.

Note that part B carries no time window at all. Money in hand against a known obligation is not a position, however far off the payment is, and forcing it to be sold and rebought would be pure waste.

Part C — the grace period

Scenario S07: an account that simply opens holding a lot of one currency.

This is the part you are most likely to meet without expecting it, and the one most likely to explain a surprising answer.

An account opens with 2,500,000 dollars. The only dollar obligation all week is 50,000 on day 5. So parts A and B allow just 50,000 — and the account is already holding fifty times that, before the model gets a say.

S07 — USDD0D1D2D3D4D5
Do-nothing ladder2,500,0002,500,0002,500,000 2,500,0002,500,0002,450,000
A — to acquire000 000
B — earmark50,00050,00050,000 50,00050,00050,000
C — grace2,500,0002,500,0000 000
CEILING2,500,0002,500,00050,000 50,00050,00050,000

Without part C the ceiling would be 50,000 on day 0 — and the account is holding 2,500,000 on day 0 as a matter of fact, not of choice. The model would have no legal starting position at all, or would be forced into a same-day sale on day nought with no choice of tenor.

So the grace admits the balance that was already there, but only for the first few days. After that the ordinary ceiling applies and the surplus must be gone.

Why the grace ends when it does

The reasoning is: money on the books at the start could not have been sold before day 0, and the longest settlement your desk offers takes two days. So two days is exactly long enough to exit at any tenor you like — and not a day more. Narrowing it would force a same-day sale and remove the choice of tenor; widening it would permit holding for no reason.

The deadline is on the settlement day, not the dealing day. This catches people out. You have until day 2 for the money to be gone, not until day 2 to deal. A sale dealt on day 1 for two-day settlement lands on day 3 and breaks the rule, even though day 1 is comfortably inside the grace.

How the three combine

A and B add. C is a minimum, not an addition.

ceiling[day]  =  max(  A + B,   C  )

The distinction matters. If C were added, an account that opened holding a currency would get its opening balance plus its earmark plus its hole — a limit far larger than anything it could justify. As a minimum, C only ever rescues the case where the ordinary ceiling would be impossible to satisfy on day one.

Check it on S04 day 1: A is 0, B is 950,000, C is 1,400,000. The ceiling is 1,400,000 — C won because the receipt had just landed and had not yet been swept. By day 2 the grace has expired and the ceiling drops to the 950,000 earmark, which is what forces the 450,000 surplus out.

And on S07 day 0: A is 0, B is 50,000, C is 2,500,000, so the ceiling is 2,500,000. On day 2 C falls away and the ceiling collapses to 50,000 — a fiftyfold drop between one day and the next, which is the single most abrupt thing the corridor does.

The floor

One term, no window, no earmark. Much simpler than the ceiling.

The floor answers a different question: how overdrawn may this account go? And the answer is simply no more overdrawn than doing nothing would have made it.

floor[day]  =  the hole in the do-nothing ladder on that day

Here is S05, where a euro payment of 1,600,000 lands three days before the receipt meant to cover it:

S05 — EURD0D1D2D3D4D5
Do-nothing ladder00−1,600,000 −1,600,000−1,600,000150,000
FLOOR00−1,600,000 −1,600,000−1,600,0000
CEILING001,600,000 1,600,0001,600,0000

Doing nothing lands you exactly on the floor, and any trading can only lift you off it. So the floor forbids nothing the account could genuinely experience. What it forbids is an overdraft the model would have to manufacture — selling a currency it never owned in order to be short of it.

Why the floor exists at all

Being short a currency is the same speculation as being long, in reverse. Sell a low-yielding currency you do not own and the interest differential accrues to you exactly as it would if you had bought a high-yielding one. A ceiling with no floor would forbid one and permit the other.

Note that the ceiling and the floor are separate limits on separate things, not two ends of one range that the balance slides along. The balance is split into a positive part and a negative part, the ceiling caps the positive part, the floor caps the negative part, and only one of them can be non-zero on any given day.

One consequence: the ceiling does not stop an account going overdrawn. That is the floor's job, and the floor is deliberately generous. The model prices overdrafts as a real cost and is often right to run one.

The one-unit slack

Both limits are applied about one currency unit looser than they read.

A limit sitting exactly on the value a plan needs is one the solver cannot always certify, and a scenario was observed flipping to "no feasible plan" over a hundredth of a currency unit. So both limits get a small amount of slack — the larger of one currency unit or one part in a million:

applied limit  =  limit  +  max( limit × 0.000001,   1.0 )

So a ceiling that reads 750,000 is applied at 750,001. Slack is only added where the limit is already positive: a limit of zero stays exactly zero, so a currency with nothing to justify still cannot hold a penny.

A known consequence

The model will use that slack if using it is free. In one scenario it holds one dollar more than the round number — because the extra dollar earns a fraction of a penny in interest and costs nothing, both pieces of the split ticket falling in the same commission band — and then has to sell that single dollar on the last day to satisfy the end-of-week sweep. The plan is correct and the trade is absurd. Setting a minimum ticket size removes it.

The general lesson: a numerical tolerance placed on a rule to keep the solver stable is readable by the optimiser as genuine economic headroom.

Interactions worth knowing

Where two parts are live at once, and what happens.

Both limits active in one week

S22 runs a dollar balance in and out of overdraft four times. Different days are governed by different parts, and both limits do work:

S22 — USDD0D1D2D3D4D5
Do-nothing ladder0−800,000700,000 −200,000−200,000200,000
A — to acquire0800,0000 200,000200,0000
B — earmark00900,000 900,00000
CEILING0800,000900,000 1,100,000200,0000
FLOOR0−800,0000 −200,000−200,0000

Day 3 is the interesting one: the ceiling is 1,100,000, made of 200,000 you still have to buy plus 900,000 of the day-2 receipt that the day-3 payment will consume. Neither part alone would allow it, and the account genuinely might hold that much legitimately.

The grace can be the only thing keeping a scenario feasible

Where an account opens holding more than its obligations justify, removing the grace leaves it illegal on day 0 by construction — before any decision is made. It would then have to sell same-day on day nought, and if the desk does not quote same-day settlement there is no legal plan at all.

The grace is worth real money where interest runs in your favour

On S07 each extra day of grace is worth about 158 in interest, because the account is holding 2,500,000 dollars at a rate well above the base currency. Shorten the grace and the cost rises; lengthen it and the cost falls, in a straight line. On a scenario with no unearmarked opening balance the grace does nothing at all.

So the grace is a speculation allowance, measured in days

Where a currency yields more than base and the account holds more of it than it needs, the grace period is precisely the window in which the model may keep collecting that difference. That is worth knowing before changing it, because it is the one part of the corridor that directly trades policy against money.

How each is set

What you can configure, what is derived, and what is fixed.

PieceHow it is setCan you change it?
The corridor as a whole The holding_ceiling switch. Off, and both limits disappear. Yes — a flag, and a toggle on the web page
Part A's look-ahead settle_on_need_only. On (the default) it looks only at the day itself. Off, it looks ahead by the longest settlement lag. Yes — a flag
Part B, the earmark Entirely from the cash flows. Receipts banked, capped by payments remaining. No — change the cash flows
Part C, the grace The longest settlement lag your tenor list contains. With T0, T1, T2 that is two days. Not directly — see below
The floor Entirely from the cash flows. The deepest hole in the ladder. No — change the cash flows
The slack holding_min_slack (1.0) and holding_tolerance (one in a million). Yes, but they are numerical settings, not policy

Two of the six are pure consequences of the data — the earmark and the floor cannot be set at all, only changed by changing what the account expects to pay and receive. Two are proper policy switches. One is a numerical tolerance. And one, the grace period, is neither: it is derived from something else entirely.

The coupling problem

The grace period is set by your tenor list, and that has a consequence nobody chose.

The grace period is not a setting. It is read off the longest settlement lag in the tenor list — a piece of market data describing what your desk can deal. So adding a tenor to that list lengthens the grace period, whether or not anyone intended it and whether or not the tenor is ever used.

On S07, adding tenors that the model never deals:

Tenors offeredGraceCostInterest collectedTrade actually dealt
T0, T1, T2 (as shipped)2 days451.71333.13sell on day 2, same-day

Every one of those plans deals a single same-day trade. T3, T4 and T5 are never used — they exist only as entries in a list. Each one buys a day of legal holding worth about 158.

And the last row is the part that matters

A cost of −23.72 with a five-day tenor declared is identical to the cost with the anti-speculation policy switched off entirely. Declaring a long enough tenor neutralises the rule completely — and the flag summary still reports the policy as active, because as far as it is concerned it is.

So the route to disabling a risk control runs through the market data rather than through the risk settings. Those are usually different approval paths.

In practice this only bites where an account opens holding more of a currency than its obligations justify — three of the forty currency-scenarios in the shipped library. But that is the ordinary shape of a real book on a Monday morning, and it includes the one scenario written specifically to test this policy.

The fix is small: make the grace period a setting in its own right, defaulting to the longest settlement lag so that nothing changes unless someone chooses it. That would let you offer three-day settlement while keeping a two-day grace, or vice versa, which are genuinely separate decisions.

Quick reference

−floor[day]  ≤  balance[day]  ≤  ceiling[day]

ceiling[day] = max( A + B, C )
  A = the hole on this day  (or the worst hole within the settlement window, if settle_on_need_only is off)
  B = min( payments still ahead, money received so far )
  C = the do-nothing balance, for the first grace days only

floor[day]   = the hole on this day

both limits applied + max( limit × 0.000001, 1.0 ), and only where the limit is above zero
If you see…Look at…
A currency it refuses to hold at all early in the week Part A. With the default setting the ceiling is zero until the day the money leaves.
A receipt it keeps rather than sweeping Part B. It is earmarked against a payment still ahead.
An opening balance it lets you keep for a couple of days, then not Part C. The grace has expired.
A hand-entered plan rejected for a balance on a day with no cash flow The settlement day, not the dealing day. The limit applies to where the money lands.
An overdraft it is happy to run Not the ceiling's business. The floor permits any overdraft the cash flows themselves dig.
A trade of one currency unit The slack. Harmless, absurd, and removed by setting a minimum ticket.
"No feasible plan" on an account that clearly has money Turn one switch off and re-solve. Whichever restores feasibility was the binding one.

Every ladder, limit and cost on this page was produced by solving the shipped scenario library, not worked out by hand. Where a figure is quoted it can be reproduced by loading that scenario and solving it.

The fastest way to find out which rule is driving an answer is still to switch one off and solve again. The whole sweep takes under a second.
