Sweeping the Surplus

A proposed rule: unearmarked foreign credit must be dealt on day 0, at any tenor. Should it cover only the opening balance, or later receipts too? Measured across the whole scenario library and five purpose-built stress cases.

Findings first

If you read nothing else.

  1. 01 The two variants are almost the same rule Across twenty-five scenarios, variant A changes one plan and variant B changes two. The extra scenario costs 2.94 more. Whatever you were expecting the difference to be, it is smaller.
  2. 02 Because the corridor already sweeps later receipts on arrival A receipt landing on day 2 or later gets no grace at all — the ceiling drops on the very day it arrives. Only receipts landing inside the grace window are left loose, and with the shipped tenors that window is day 1 alone. That is the entire extra scope of variant B.
  3. 03 The cost is small and lands in one place S07 goes from 451.71 to 484.46 under either variant — the whole of the change, and it buys the fix for the rolling problem. Everything else in the library is untouched or moves by pennies.
  4. 04 Variant B removes the one-dollar artefact on S04 The split ticket of 449,999 plus 1 collapses into a single clean 450,000 sale. Not a reason to choose B on its own, but a real side benefit.
  5. 05 Skipping the rule below the minimum ticket changes nothing It is the right decision and it is inert. Whenever a surplus is below the minimum ticket, the corridor's ceiling has already made the scenario infeasible. My rule standing down does not rescue it.
  6. 06 Only variant B fully closes the rolling problem A fixes it for opening balances. A day-1 receipt would still be able to roll, because it is the one case where money sits unearmarked with a deadline measured from today.

What this rule is for

A plan that is optimal every morning and never executed.

The corridor stops the model holding currency it cannot justify. But it says when the position must be gone, not when the decision must be made. And the deadline is measured from today.

So a plan can say "sell in two days", and tomorrow, re-planned, say "sell in two days" again. The deadline moves with you. Rolling a book forward day by day, re-planning each morning and following the plan faithfully, the position is never sold:

Each plan is individually correct. The sequence is not. And it is not a coin-flip the model occasionally resolves in your favour — deferring is strictly cheaper by about 16.70 a day, so it defers every time.

The proposed fix is to constrain the decision rather than the balance: any unearmarked foreign credit must be dealt on day 0, at whatever tenor you like. Once contracted, tomorrow's re-plan has nothing left to defer.

Why not just shorten the grace period?

Because the grace is a limit on the balance. Setting it to zero forces settlement on day 0, which means same-day dealing and nothing else — it removes the tenor choice entirely and is infeasible outright if your desk does not quote T+0. It costs about 290 more than letting the model pick its tenor. Shortening the grace changes when the deadline falls; it cannot change what the deadline is anchored to.

The two variants

Variant A — opening balance only

Whatever unearmarked foreign credit is in the opening balance must be sold on day 0, at any tenor. Money arriving later is left to the corridor.

Variant B — receipts too

Whatever unearmarked foreign credit is on the books on any day must have been sold by that day, at any tenor. A receipt that arrives spare must be dealt the day it lands.

Both are constraints on trades rather than on balances, which makes them the first corridor rules of their kind. Everything else in the corridor works by making a balance impossible; these force a trade to exist.

Both leave the tenor free. That matters: it is the difference between a plan that costs 484.46 and one that costs 768.66 on the same book.

Measuring the surplus

One obvious definition is wrong, and expensively so.

"Unearmarked" means: on the books, and not needed for any payment still ahead in that currency.

surplus[day]  =  max( 0,  do-nothing balance[day]  −  earmark[day]  )

The measure has to be taken against the balance you actually hold, not against cumulative receipts. I first wrote it as "receipts banked so far, less debts still ahead", which reads plausibly and is badly wrong: a receipt that arrives to repay an overdraft is not spare cash, but that formula counts it as though it were.

What that error did

On S05 — where a euro payment of 1.6m lands three days before the 1.75m receipt meant to cover it — the bad measure read the entire receipt as surplus and forced it all to be sold. Cost went from 760.20 to 4,290.12. On S22, which whipsaws in and out of overdraft, it forced a sale on nearly every day and cost nine times the correct answer.

Every number elsewhere on this page uses the corrected measure. The wrong one is recorded because it is an easy mistake to repeat, and because the symptom — a rule that looks reasonable producing costs several times too high — is worth recognising.

Why B adds so little

The most useful thing on this page. The corridor is already doing most of B's job.

The grace period covers the first two days only. A receipt arriving after that gets no grace at all — the ceiling drops on the very day it lands, so the corridor already forces it out immediately. There is nothing left for variant B to add.

The same receipt of 900,000, arriving on different days:

So the entire difference between the two variants is one day

Variant A covers surplus present on day 0. The corridor already covers surplus arriving on day 2 or later. That leaves exactly the days inside the grace window but after day 0 — with the shipped tenors, day 1 and nothing else.

Which is precisely why S04, whose receipt lands on day 1, is the only scenario in the library where the variants disagree.

One consequence worth noting: the gap widens if you ever offer longer tenors. A three-day settlement makes the grace three days, so receipts landing on days 1 and 2 would be left loose, and variant B would cover two days rather than one.

Case three is worth a second look. The book opens holding 600,000 with a further 900,000 arriving on day 2. Both variants force a day-0 deal, and the model sells 1,400,000 — more than it holds on day 0 — for settlement on day 2, by which time the receipt has landed. That is legal, sensible, and cheaper than two tickets, because commission tiers restart on every ticket. The rule does not confine you to selling what is in the account today.

The minimum-ticket decision

You chose to skip the rule when the surplus is too small to deal. It is right, and it does nothing.

The concern was that a surplus smaller than the minimum ticket would make the rule demand a trade nobody could execute, turning a workable scenario infeasible. Skipping the rule in that case avoids it. But by the time the rule would have fired, the corridor has already caused the same failure:

The ceiling drops to 100,000 on day 2 regardless, so a 20,000 sale is required whether or not the new rule asks for one — and 20,000 is far below the 632,911 minimum. The scenario was already infeasible.

So keep the skip, but do not expect it to help

It is still the correct behaviour: the rule should not be the thing that breaks a book, and the skip makes the new rule strictly weaker than the corridor it sits beside. It just will not rescue anything, because the corridor gets there first. If small unearmarked balances against a large minimum ticket are a real situation for you, the fix belongs in the corridor, not here.

Receipt landing on day 2 or laterno — ceiling bites on arrivaln/an/a

Variant A leaves one hole: a receipt landing on day 1 is unearmarked, gets a day of grace, and that grace is measured from today. Re-plan tomorrow and the same receipt — now an opening balance — is covered by variant A after all, so in practice it rolls once and is then caught.

That is a much smaller leak than the opening-balance case, which rolls forever. But it is a leak, and variant B closes it for the cost of 2.94 on one library scenario.

How I would choose

Given how close the two are, the decision is not really about cost. It is about which rule is easier to explain and harder to get wrong.

Variant A — opening only

Simpler to state: "what you start the week holding and do not need, you deal today." One constraint per currency, evaluated once.

Leaves the day-1 receipt able to roll once. Changes one library plan.

Easier to reconcile against a desk's own daily process, which usually starts from the opening position.

Variant B — receipts too

Harder to state precisely, because "the day it becomes available" needs the surplus measured correctly on every day — the trap in section four.

Closes the leak completely. Removes the one-dollar artefact on S04. Changes two library plans.

One constraint per currency per day, so more rows; immaterial at this size.

I would take variant B, on the grounds that the extra scope costs 2.94 across the entire library and closes the rule's only remaining gap — and a rule with a known hole in it is harder to reason about later than a rule without one. The measurement trap is a one-time implementation risk, not an ongoing one, and it is now documented.

The case for A is real though, and it is about people rather than money: "sweep what you opened with" is a sentence a treasurer can hold in their head, and it matches how a desk already thinks about the start of the day. If this rule is going to be explained to anyone who does not read the model, that is worth more than 2.94.

Whichever you pick, one thing has to be true around it

The optimiser has no memory of contracted trades. Deal T+2 on day 0, re-plan on day 1, and the day-1 run still sees the money on the books with no idea a sale is already booked — so it would demand another one. This rule is only safe if your rolling process feeds pending settlements into the next run's ladder. That is what the integration layer's day-over-day balance deltas are for, but it should be confirmed before either variant goes near production.

Every figure here comes from solving the model with the constraint added, not from estimating what it would do. The experiment rebuilds the optimiser and adds the rule as an extra constraint; no package code was changed to produce these numbers.

The corridor itself — ceiling, floor, grace and how they interact — is documented separately in docs/holding-corridor.html.
